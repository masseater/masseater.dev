The cause of the string of data breaches is not the skill of engineers or old systems, but organizational structures that cannot decide to change or stop. In the October 2026 PHP2Shell case, some companies stopped before the fix was released, and some companies kept running for half a day even after confirming malicious programs.

On October 20, the PHP development team announced that it would fix two vulnerabilities rated "critical" at 23:00 Japan time the next day, the 21st, and asked all users to prepare to update while keeping the details hidden[^jpcert]. From early afternoon on the 21st, web services in Japan stopped one after another, and SAKURA internet stopped the functions of some services at 13:00[^sakura]. The Prime Minister and the National Cybersecurity Office called on X for temporarily stopping websites as an effective measure if the fix could not be applied right away. According to a tally by Assured, which runs the vulnerability management service yamory, the number of service suspension notices that could be found on X reached 502 as of 14:00 on the 23rd[^mynavi]. Each company's response is collected in [the list by the Web Security Assessment Center](https://websec-center.com/blog/kokunai-kigyou-taiou/).

The same week, at GMO Internet's 「お名前.com デスクトップクラウド」, a VPS for automated FX trading, it took half a day from confirming malicious programs to stopping all servers, and users suffered losses in the meantime. According to the company's announcement, it began investigating on the 19th after an inquiry from a customer, confirmed on the 20th that malicious programs had been placed on customers' servers, and stopped all 26,739 servers across all plans at 4:00 on the 21st[^gmo-1]. In its second report on the 22nd, it admitted that login information for trading accounts stored on the servers may have been obtained by a third party and used without authorization[^gmo-2]. In the account of a man in Kanagawa Prefecture reported by the Asahi Shimbun, more than 4 million yen vanished in about 2 seconds on the 21st, and the damage including later trades exceeded 6 million yen[^asahi]. A woman in Aichi Prefecture had unintended trades made repeatedly in the early hours of the 22nd, after the servers had been stopped, and her funds of a little over 4.3 million yen were reportedly almost completely drained.

The organizations that were able to stop without waiting for the fix had the authority to compare the loss from stopping with the damage from an attack and decide within the day. What the organization that kept running for half a day lacked was not technology but this authority. The cause of the string of data breaches lies in the same place.

## Structures that could decide to stop

Among the cases I checked, the service in Japan that decided to stop earliest was CoDMON, a childcare ICT service. The company published a post after its response summarizing how it reached its decision, so I quote from it here[^codmon]. The company learned of the vulnerability information at 9:01 on the 21st and stopped in the 13:00 hour. The management members approved a policy of a planned stop at night during the morning, and agreement on an immediate stop had been reached all the way up to the president and the executive officer of the sales department. In the early afternoon, when it became clear that the fix method had spread and attacks could succeed before 23:00, the company used that authority to decide on an immediate stop. The company explains, 「お客さまへの影響よりも攻撃を受けた場合の被害のほうがはるかに大きいと判断した」. A structure for knowing where PHP is in the company and a management that compares loss with damage and decides moved within three and a half hours of learning the information.

In real estate portals, three competitors all stopped on the same night, but the times they stopped differed. LIFULL HOME'S entered emergency maintenance from 20:00 on the 21st, and stated in its notice that the reason was the PHP vulnerability[^lifull]. SUUMO started maintenance at 23:00 on the 21st, when the fix was released, and wrote only 「サービス向上およびシステム安定化」 as the reason in its notice. At Home also stopped various services on the night of the 21st, but its notice came out on the 22nd, and even in the evening of the 23rd, its route map search and parts of its app were still stopped[^athome]. Only one company named PHP, but since three competitors all stopped on the same night, the cause was PHP2Shell. Amazon had observed attempted attacks in its honeypots four days before the announcement[^madpot], and according to Cloudflare, tens of thousands of attacks were observed within 45 minutes of the fix's release, and millions the next day[^cloudflare-stats]. Between a company that stopped at 20:00 and one that stopped at 23:00, the time they ran their services in a state where attacks could succeed differs by three hours.

GMO kept its servers running for half a day even after confirming malicious programs, and users suffered losses in the meantime. As of 18:30 on the 20th, the company had posted a 「システムメンテナンス」 notice and told customers that their servers under contract could be used even during the maintenance[^gmo-piyolog]. The company stopped all servers at 4:00 the next day, the 21st, and told users in the evening of the 22nd that the credentials of their trading accounts may have been stolen. The funds in users' accounts vanished during this time, on the 21st and 22nd. GMO Research & AI, in the same group, had just announced two weeks earlier, on October 5, that a vulnerability in its survey site infoQ had been exploited and the personal information of all its members had been taken out[^infoq]. The intrusion route is still under investigation, so I will not discuss technical superiority here. What I discuss is the fact that, in a service directly tied to customers' assets, it took half a day from confirming malicious programs to stopping everything, and losses occurred while that decision was being made.

Organizations using PHP 8.1 or earlier, whose support had ended, had no choice other than stopping. Fixes were released only for 8.2 and later, and there is no official fix for 8.1 or earlier[^jpcert]. The remaining choices were a version upgrade taking months or staying stopped until then, and organizations that could not decide to stop could only wait to be attacked.

Each time there is an incident, the cause is sought in the skill of the front lines or in old systems, but it is not that the engineers in organizations that could not act until after the fix was released did not know how to upgrade. They were simply inside organizations that could not draw out, at the needed speed, the decision that it was all right to stop.

Incidents of intrusion through known vulnerabilities have also happened repeatedly in Japan since July 2026. On September 11, the Digital Agency announced that about 246,000 items of personal information may have leaked from GSS, the government's common work environment[^gss]. The entry point was a VPN device used for external maintenance. The Digital Agency explains that it knew of the vulnerability in advance, but it was exploited while the agency was handling other, more urgent vulnerabilities in order. The intruder stole a maintenance account and accessed a large number of files, and it took two weeks from detection on June 25 to determining that it was unauthorized access. In the National Police Agency's tally too, ransomware damage reports in the first half of 2026 numbered 123, the most ever for a half year, and VPN devices made up about half of the intrusion routes[^npa]. Attackers come in using unpatched vulnerabilities and leaked credentials, not unknown techniques. From Handa Hospital in Tokushima Prefecture, whose electronic medical records were encrypted in 2021, to GSS in 2026, the entry points were known holes for which fixes had been published.

The scale of the damage is also no longer comparable to a planned stop. On October 7, IDC Frontier announced that part of its cloud platform had been compromised by ransomware, and that the data of 495 companies and local governments would be difficult to retrieve and restore[^idcf]. At Osaka Metropolitan University, on October 2, its virtualization platform was breached and about 500 servers stopped, and many of the backups needed for restoration were also encrypted[^omu]. After a week of cancelling all classes, it is investigating whether the personal information of at least 130,000 people, including current students and graduates, went outside.

The cause lies in the development and operation structures that Japanese companies have built up over many years as "the right way to do things." These structures have treated change as a risk, made keeping the scope of impact small the goal of development, and required approvals and coordination of planned stops for releases. Operations and maintenance have had their budgets cut as an accessory to development. Under this, vulnerability response, version upgrades and emergency stops are classified on the side of "things you must not do," and with each postponement the next change grows bigger and more expensive. These structures have taken away the ability to change systems, and the responsibility for solving this also lies with management.

## Exploitation faster than human judgment

Vulnerabilities are found after software is released. Software is made with the knowledge and assumptions of the time it was written, while the attackers' knowledge keeps growing after release. Many vulnerabilities appear, as a difference in knowledge discovered later, in places nobody recognized as a problem at the time of writing. Shellshock, found in 2014, was a defect that had existed since 1989 in bash, the standard shell of UNIX-like OSes. In Log4Shell in 2021, the very feature for referencing external servers that the Java logging library Log4j had had since 2013 became the attack route. PHP2Shell is the same: a defect that had existed for more than ten years in the most basic path by which PHP receives web requests was found in 2026, and every environment from PHP 5 onward could be affected.

| Why they are found later | What happens |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Advances in attack techniques | Fuzzing and automated exploration by AI later find weaknesses that have long existed |
| Changes in assumptions | Encryption methods once considered safe are broken by improved computer performance, and systems built on the assumption of closed networks get connected to the internet |
| Inherited dependencies | Just by using them, you inherit the defects of libraries and runtimes you did not write |
| Asymmetric verification | Tests check that things work as expected, while attackers look only for what happens with unexpected inputs |

The reason the fix on the supply side was late also lay in organizational decision making. On the same day the fix was released, the PHP Foundation revealed that it had known of signs of the vulnerability at the time of an external audit in 2024[^php-foundation]. Among several fix proposals, including ones that would break compatibility and ones that would increase the risk of DoS, the small security team, which worked by consensus, could not decide, and the work stopped and restarted over several months. The foundation called this a "structural failure" and announced that it would change its decision making to majority vote. The organizations on the user side that could not decide to stop and the organization on the supplier side that could not decide on a fix were late for the same reason.

What is required of the organizations that use software is not the power to choose parts with no defects at shipping, but the ability to keep taking in the fixes that keep coming out after shipping. A Node.js application normally depends on hundreds to thousands of packages through npm, and in September 2025, a group of popular packages downloaded a total of 2 billion times a week was hijacked[^semgrep]. Just by using them, you inherit the defects of parts you did not write.

Those fixes come out in volumes beyond what people can process. In just the two months from August 4 to October 3, 2026, 27,489 CVEs, the identifiers assigned to vulnerabilities, were registered[^cve-60days]. That is 450 a day, which works out to two thirds of the annual registrations of 2024 coming out in two months. AI has entered the side of discovery. In Project Glasswing, which Anthropic started in April, the company's models found 26,153 vulnerabilities. When VulnCheck, which handles vulnerability information, tallied them in September, less than 10% could be reported to the developers, and fixes could be confirmed for 202[^vulncheck]. Against the speed of finding them, the people on the side of verifying and fixing have become the bottleneck.

The time from disclosure to exploitation has become shorter than a human meeting. In Radware's report on the first half of 2026, published in September, the average time from the disclosure of a vulnerability to the first attack shrank from 53 days in 2024 to 21.5 days in 2025, and turned to minus 8 hours in July 2026[^radware]. The share exploited on the day of disclosure or earlier exceeds 80%. In the annual report of Mandiant, part of Google, vulnerability exploitation was the entry point for 32% of intrusions, topping the list for the sixth year in a row, and the time for groups responsible for initial intrusion to hand over the environment to the next group shrank from more than 8 hours in 2022 to 22 seconds in 2025[^m-trends]. With PHP2Shell, attack attempts started four days before the announcement and reached tens of thousands within 45 minutes of the fix's release, and this course of events is just one example of this trend.

The automation of attacks has also been observed in Japan. On October 9, JPCERT/CC issued an alert about the string of unauthorized access incidents in Japan, and in its update on the 23rd added attacks that abuse AI-based penetration testing platforms as one of the techniques[^jpcert-0030]. These automatically probe public web applications for various vulnerabilities, attempt to exploit them, and steal configuration files. In Macnica's tally, announcements of personal information leaks from the web systems of Japanese corporations numbered 62 in 2024 and 84 in 2025, while in 2026 they reached 119 by October 6, with 81 of them concentrated after July[^macnica]. As long as everything from generating attack code to searching for targets, intruding and spreading inside chains together automatically, defenders must make decisions on the assumption that attackers with no limit on their numbers start moving at the same moment as disclosure.

A structure that takes days to decide is structurally too late, however good its technology. Incidents of intrusion through known vulnerabilities have long happened in the same form, but now fixes come out in volumes beyond human processing capacity, and the time from disclosure to exploitation has become shorter than a human meeting. While people call a meeting, investigate the scope of impact and circulate an approval request on whether to stop, the attack is already over.

## Structures that see change as evil

Most of the work of system development begins after the system is finished. It is not unusual for a business system to live more than ten years, and during that time, updates to the OS and middleware, replacements of libraries, vulnerability responses and keeping up with legal revisions continue. Seen over the whole life cycle, the process of building is only 20%, and the remaining 80% is the process of keeping it running. The Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry's DX Report also pointed out that about 80% of companies' IT-related costs go to maintaining and operating existing systems[^meti-dx].

Many Japanese companies have called this 80% "maintenance" and treated it as an accessory to development. Budgets are attached to development projects, the team is scaled down as soon as the system is finished, and maintenance becomes a job of fixing things when they break with the minimum number of people and cost. Vulnerability response occurs exactly on the side of this 80%.

Meanwhile, the language runtimes the systems run on grow old by themselves. The support periods of language runtimes such as PHP, Node.js, Ruby and Perl are roughly two and a half to four years per version, and once they pass, no more fixes are released. A system used for ten years works out to being forced into two or three major version upgrades along the way.

Even for the same vulnerability response, the cost differs by orders of magnitude between within the support period and after it has ended. Within the support period, a vulnerability response takes only a few hours of applying a patch, and behavior does not change. After support has ended, the same response turns into a months-long project of crushing incompatibilities in the language specification, replacing all dependency libraries and retesting every feature. In the meantime, new vulnerabilities come out again. The organizations using PHP 8.1 or earlier at the time of PHP2Shell were in this situation: there was no patch, and a version upgrade would take months, so they could only stop. Many of the PHP 5 and 7 systems, Perl CGI and old Rails applications remaining in Japan are also things whose cost of change swelled to an unpayable size as postponements piled up. Neglect is not maintaining the status quo.

Work on a running system costs something apart from the work itself.

| Cost | Details |
| -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The cost of stopping the service | Coordinating the schedule of a planned stop with customers and related departments, announcing it, and absorbing the impact on business |
| The cost of confirming nothing is broken | Verifying in a test environment and checking that it works in production |
| The cost of getting internal approval | Taking it to a change management committee, circulating approval requests and getting the agreement of those involved |

The development and operation structures of Japanese companies have been built in the direction of making these three thoroughly heavy. Change management procedures exist to prevent failures caused by changes, and advance notice of planned stops is consideration for customers. The habit of splitting pull requests small and localizing fixes so as not to touch existing behavior also makes review easier and incidents less likely. Each of these is right in itself.

The problem is that all of these are piled up on the premise that "change is a bad thing." In organizations where reducing change and containing its impact has become the goal, nobody invests in mechanisms that lower the cost of change. Automated tests are not built, deploys rely on procedure documents and visual checks, and the people who know the whole picture of the system dwindle. If development aimed only at keeping the scope of impact small continues, fixes with a large scope of impact, such as updating frameworks or reviewing the architecture, will never be done. Almost everything Japanese companies have typically done falls into this anti-pattern.

All three costs swell in proportion to the time since the last change, so compound interest works here. The longer a system has gone untouched, the larger the difference, the longer the stop and the heavier the approval. Because it is heavy, it is postponed, and because it is postponed, the next time is even heavier. Meanwhile, the people who understood the system leave, and the test code rots. A structure designed to prevent failures caused by change is turning change itself into the most dangerous act. The circumstances in which known vulnerabilities were waiting in line at the Digital Agency, and the inside of the organizations that could not decide to stop before the PHP2Shell fix was released, are inside the same compound interest.

## Tools that lower the cost of change

If you split changes into small everyday units, the coordination of stops and approvals is no longer needed. Tools such as Dependabot and Renovate detect updates to dependency libraries, automatically create pull requests, and deploy them to production if automated tests pass in CI. However, the premise is that automated tests exist; without tests, the cost of confirming nothing is broken does not disappear, and automated updates do not work. As DORA's research has repeatedly shown, organizations with higher deploy frequency also have lower change failure rates[^dora]. Things are safe because they change often, not because they do not change.

If you let go of the lower layers, the amount of change you have to handle drops greatly. If you use databases and application platforms provided by cloud providers, vulnerability responses for the OS, middleware and runtime are done on the provider's side. With PHP2Shell too, SAKURA internet applied the fix to the PHP it provides, and users did not need to do anything[^sakura]. Cloudflare delivered its own WAF rules to all users, including the free plan, about 9 hours before the fix was released, and began blocking attacks on the sites under it[^cloudflare-waf]. Above the boundary of the shared responsibility model, that is, the application code, the dependency libraries and the choice of the runtime's major version, the responsibility remains with the company.

Letting go is not cutting corners. Precisely because it is an era in which AI makes it look as if you can do everything yourself, deliberately creating parts you decide not to own, and reducing the situations where human judgment is needed, becomes the design. Since a structure where people watch every layer against attackers who work 24 hours a day cannot work, the only way is to increase the layers you do not have to watch.

A WAF is not a fix but a means of buying time until the fix is applied. It is a mechanism that blocks attack patterns in front of the application, so Cloudflare could block attacks even before the fix was released. However, a WAF only buys time without paying any of the three costs, and the vulnerability remains. The organizations most at risk are those where "we're fine because we have a WAF" has become an excuse to justify postponing patches and version upgrades.

Whatever tools you choose, the decisions remain: when to stop the service, how much to invest in testing and automation, which layers to let go of, and how much risk to accept. Only management can make these decisions.

## The asymmetry in how losses appear

The reason the front lines cannot decide to stop is an asymmetry in how losses appear. The loss from a planned stop is visible. You know how much sales will drop from stopping for an hour, how many people customer support will take, and whose responsibility it will be. The loss from a breach is invisible. You do not know when it will happen, the amount is not fixed until it happens, and whose responsibility it is remains vague. When a certain loss is weighed against an uncertain loss, "let's pass this time" is always the rational choice for an individual. The company's irrationality is born from piled-up individual rationality, and the cause is not the negligence of the people in charge.

The invisible losses are not small. IDC Frontier's 495 customers lost their data unless they had their own backups. Osaka Metropolitan University stopped classes for a week, and GMO's users lost millions of yen from their own accounts. In the National Police Agency's survey, 60% of organizations hit by ransomware spent a total of 10 million yen or more on recovery, and less than half were able to recover in under a month[^npa]. As a result of postponing work that would have taken a few hours in a planned stop, unplanned stops continue for weeks to months.

Many organizations have not even noticed on their own that they have been breached. In Mandiant's report, 52% of organizations were able to detect breaches on their own in 2025, and the rest learned of them through outside notification[^m-trends]. When Macnica analyzed 81 announcements in Japan since July, 65 lacked enough explanation to classify the cause of the intrusion[^macnica]. At the time of announcing a leak, 80% of organizations cannot explain where they were entered. For organizations that do not keep logs, that keep them but do not look at them, or that have no criteria for judging anomalies, "we have not been affected" is nothing more than the flip side of not being able to detect it.

Even after you apply a patch, you cannot drive out attackers who got in before it was applied. With PHP2Shell, attacks had started before the announcement, so JPCERT/CC asked organizations to check for breaches before the fix was applied, at the same time as applying it[^jpcert]. In the GMO incident, funds vanished from users' accounts even after the servers were stopped. Stolen credentials remain valid until users change them themselves. It is not hard work for intruders to plant web shells or other routes of intrusion and erase their traces from the logs, so attackers stay inside organizations that feel safe after applying patches. The months after a fix is released are the most dangerous, and organizations without a structure to check have no way of meeting JPCERT/CC's request.

The estimate that "we have no data that would be a problem if stolen" is also wrong. JPCERT/CC's alert gives examples of systems not meant for use by the general public, such as BI tools and management systems for employees, being breached and internal information leaking[^jpcert-0030]. A hijacked server, regardless of the data it stores, is used as a stepping stone for attacks on other organizations, a place for phishing sites, or a source for distributing malware. Once the company name comes out not as a victim but as infrastructure on the attacker's side, the trust of business partners is lost, and in some cases legal responsibility is also questioned.

The responsibility for accurately recognizing the size of the risk of being attacked lies not with the engineering organization but with management. At a minimum, management must be able to answer the following with numbers.

- Which of the company's systems face the internet
- Which systems run on parts whose support has ended
- How many days they would stop and how much would be lost if breached
- To whom reporting obligations would arise

Executives who do not grasp these in numbers are not accepting the risk; they simply do not know it. A risk you do not know cannot be managed.

The result of each company rationally piling up postponements is the industry-scale technical debt that the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry called the "2025 digital cliff" in 2018. The ministry estimated the economic loss from leaving legacy systems in place at up to 12 trillion yen a year from 2025 onward[^meti-dx]. The debt the DX Report saw from the development side and the vulnerabilities this post sees from the attacker's side are the same thing. The engineering organization's problem is the company's problem, and the company's problem has become the industry's problem.

## Management that stands on the side of cutting losses

Management does not decide whether to respond to each individual vulnerability. It decides whether to place the company on the side that keeps reducing change and lets debt grow with compound interest, or on the side that makes change everyday and builds up capability with compound interest. As long as the company is on the former side, no matter how good the engineers it hires, it will be too late.

The reason to take the certain loss of a planned stop before a breach that may or may not happen lies in the asymmetry between attackers and defenders. Penetration tests and vulnerability assessments are bound by the scope of the client's consent, responsible disclosure and the Unauthorized Computer Access Act. Real attackers are bound by nothing and do not choose their time. Since dealing with things after being attacked structurally cannot win, the only way is to request assessments yourself before being attacked and deal with the weaknesses found before damage occurs. Stopping services, discarding old systems and rebuilding them all come with losses, but after damage occurs, it is too late.

Defenders also have social responsibility. Companies hold their users' personal information and the secrecy of their communications, and the Act on the Protection of Personal Information requires reports to the supervisory authority and notification of the individuals concerned when leaks occur. The ones to whom companies owe that duty are the users and society. Sales lost in a planned stop can be won back the next month, but leaked information and lost social trust do not come back. 「承認が下りなかった」 and 「停止の調整がつかなかった」 may pass inside the company, but they do not pass in society. The basis for cutting losses is not a comparison of amounts but the responsibility of being the one who holds the data.

However, defenders also need restraint. WAFs and log monitoring are also acts of inspecting the contents of communications, so they must be kept within the scope of legitimate business in relation to the secrecy of communications. The ethics required of defenders is to act before damage occurs, within the bounds of restraint.

Delegate emergency authority in advance. If you decide in advance who may stop what, build slots for emergency changes and windows for regular maintenance into contracts and SLAs, and define the range in which stops and changes can be made without approval requests, then on the day, you only need to carry them out. CoDMON had agreement on the authority for an immediate stop all the way up to the president by the morning, so it was able to stop in three and a half hours.

Invest constantly in the ability to change. Automated tests, CI/CD, automated dependency updates, configuration management and detection mechanisms are the foundation that supports the 80% of the period spent keeping things running. So build them into the annual budget as operating costs, and do not separate the budgets for development and security. The two are the same work that uses the same ability.

Make risks visible and report them regularly. Bring to the board of directors, with amounts and deadlines, the list of systems running on parts whose support has ended, the serious vulnerabilities not yet addressed, the expected number of days of stoppage and amount of loss in case of a breach, and whether reporting obligations apply, and if you decide not to respond, record that as an explicit management decision. In short, this is also what the "Cybersecurity Management Guidelines" of the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry and IPA ask of executives[^meti-guideline].

The organizations that stopped their services on October 21, 2026, without waiting for the fix to be released, did not have special technology; they had a structure in which they knew where PHP was in the company, compared the loss from stopping with the damage from being attacked, and management decided within the day. Whether the same decision can be made on the day the next vulnerability is announced is decided by the structure management has built up to today.
